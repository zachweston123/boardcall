What a Surfboard Recommendation App Should Actually Do

If you're searching for a surfboard recommendation app, there's a good chance you've already tried the generic kind — answer five quiz questions about your height and experience, get told to buy a "7'2\" funboard," and never hear from the app again once you actually own boards. That's not a recommendation. That's a shopping quiz wearing a surf app's clothing.

A surfboard recommendation app that's actually useful day to day has to answer a different question: given the boards you already own, and what the ocean is doing at your break this morning, which one should you grab? Answering that well takes two things a static quiz can't provide — real volume math run against your real quiver, and real, current wave, wind, and tide data, not a one-time guess made the day you installed the app.

Below is the actual math and the actual guidance: how surfboard volume is calculated, why bigger waves need more volume rather than less, and how to read wetsuit thickness for real water temperatures. Then we'll show exactly how Boardcall runs all of it against your own quiver, automatically, every morning.

Why Generic "What Surfboard Should I Get" Quizzes Miss the Point

Most things that call themselves a surfboard recommendation app are really a one-time buying quiz. You answer a handful of questions — height, weight, experience level — and it spits out a single suggestion, usually just a length and a board type, meant to help you shop for your next board. Once you own it, the app has nothing left to say to you.

That's a different job from what most surfers need on any given day: it's not "what board should I buy," it's "which of the boards I already own should I paddle out on this morning." Answering that second question well requires two things a static quiz can't provide:

  • Your boards' real dimensions — not a generic "shortboard" or "longboard" label, but the actual length, width, and thickness of the specific boards in your garage, converted into real volume.
  • Today's actual conditions — wave face height, swell period, and wind at your specific break, not a one-time snapshot from whenever you downloaded the app.

Without both, a "recommendation" is really just a guess dressed up as a calculation.

The Volume Math Behind a Real Surfboard Recommendation

Surfboard volume — measured in liters — is the single number that most determines whether a board will actually work for you in a given wave, more than length or a "shortboard vs. longboard" label alone. Two 6'0" boards can differ by 8+ liters depending on width and thickness, and that difference is often the entire reason one paddles into everything and the other doesn't.

You can estimate any board's volume yourself. Multiply length × width × thickness (all in inches) to get a rough "box" volume in cubic inches, convert to liters, then apply a shape factor to account for rails, rocker, and deck contour — a board is never a solid rectangular block, so the raw box number always overstates the real volume:

Board typeTypical shape factor
Soft-top0.60
Longboard0.58
Mid-length0.55
Fish0.54
Groveler0.53
Shortboard0.50
Step-up0.49
Gun0.47

Worked example — a 6'0" × 19.5" × 2.5" shortboard: 72 × 19.5 × 2.5 = 3,510 cubic inches. Convert to liters (× 0.0163871) and you get roughly 57.5 "box" liters. Apply the 0.50 shortboard shape factor and you land at about 28.8 liters — which lines up almost exactly with real-world stamped volumes on boards of that size.

Run the same math on a 9'0" × 22.75" × 3.0" longboard: about 120.8 box liters × 0.58 = roughly 70 liters, again right in line with what longboard shapers actually stamp on a board that size.

How Much Volume You Actually Need — and the Big-Wave Paradox

Once you know a board's volume, the next question is how much you need. A reasonable rule of thumb, used widely in surf coaching, ties target volume to your body weight and skill level:

Skill levelRough target volumeExample at 75kg (165lb)
Beginnerbodyweight (kg) × ~0.9–1.068–75 L
Intermediatebodyweight (kg) × ~0.55–0.6541–49 L
Advancedbodyweight (kg) × ~0.40–0.4530–34 L
Expertbodyweight (kg) × ~0.33–0.3825–28 L

That baseline then has to move with the conditions, and this is where a lot of surfers get it backwards. It's intuitive that small, weak surf needs more volume — extra foam helps you catch anything at all when there's not much push. What's counter-intuitive is that big waves need more volume too, not less. Getting into a wave early, before it's too steep and fast to catch, takes paddle power — and paddle power comes from volume. That's exactly why a dedicated big-wave gun is a bigger board than your everyday shortboard, not a smaller one, even though it's also narrower and less maneuverable.

Roughly, here's how board type maps to wave size once you factor that in:

ConditionsWorks wellStruggles
Small & weak (knee-high or under, short period)Longboard, soft-top, fishShortboard, step-up, gun
Small & clean (long-period groundswell)Fish, groveler, mid-lengthGun, step-up
Waist to chest highShortboard, fish, mid-lengthLongboard, gun
OverheadStep-up, shortboardLongboard, soft-top
Well overhead / double overhead+Gun, step-upEverything else

Swell period matters as much as height here. A 2-foot wave at a 16-second period carries far more energy and pushes far harder than a 2-foot wave at an 8-second period — which is why wave height alone, without period, is an incomplete picture of what a board actually needs to handle.

Wetsuit Thickness by Water Temperature

The right board doesn't matter much if you're too cold to stay out and use it. Wetsuit thickness is usually written as two numbers in millimeters — torso / limb thickness (e.g. "4/3") — and the right choice comes down to water temperature, not air temperature:

Water tempSuit
75°F+ (24°C+)Boardshorts or a rash guard — no wetsuit needed
70–75°F (21–24°C)Rash guard, or a 2mm top/spring suit for longer sessions
65–70°F (18–21°C)2/2mm or 3/2mm spring suit or shorty
62–65°F (17–18°C)3/2mm full suit
58–63°F (14–17°C)4/3mm full suit, boots optional
52–58°F (11–14°C)4/3mm or 5/4mm full suit, hood optional, boots recommended
44–52°F (7–11°C)5/4mm or 6/5mm hooded full suit, boots and gloves
Below 44°F (below 7°C)6/5/4mm hooded suit with boots and gloves, or drysuit territory

These ranges overlap on purpose — a lean surfer runs colder than a heavier one in the same water, and a long session in wind always feels colder than a short one at the same temperature. Treat them as a starting point, not a rule.

Quick answers

Is there an app that recommends a surfboard based on the boards you already own?

Yes. Boardcall ranks the boards you actually enter into your quiver — by real length × width × thickness → volume, not a generic size chart — against today's wave height, period, wind, and your skill level, and names the one to bring, plus why.

How much surfboard volume do I need?

As a rough baseline, multiply your body weight in kilograms by about 0.9–1.0 if you're a beginner, 0.55–0.65 if you're intermediate, 0.40–0.45 if you're advanced, or 0.33–0.38 if you're an expert — then add volume for small, weak surf or for overhead-plus waves, and use slightly less for clean, mid-size conditions.

Do bigger waves need a bigger or smaller surfboard?

Bigger, counter-intuitively. Catching a wave early enough to be safe and in control takes paddle power, and paddle power comes from volume — which is why a dedicated big-wave gun carries more volume than an everyday shortboard, even though it's narrower and thinner.
