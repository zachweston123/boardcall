Free Surfline Alternative: What You Actually Get for $0

If you're searching "free surfline alternative," you're probably staring at a paywall right now — a live cam that cuts to a subscribe prompt after a few minutes, a forecast that only shows today and tomorrow, or a login screen between you and the swell chart — and wondering if there's a way to check the surf without paying $8–10 a month. There is, but the honest answer depends on which part of Surfline you actually use.

If it's the live cams, no free product genuinely replaces that, and we'll say so plainly rather than bury it three sections down. If it's the actual forecast-and-decision part of Surfline — what's the swell doing, is the wind clean, is it even worth the drive, what board should I bring — free alternatives get you most of the way there, and at least one of them gives you more than Surfline's own free tier does for that specific job, at no cost, ever.

Below: exactly what Surfline locks behind its paywall, where the raw weather and tide data actually comes from (it's the same handful of public sources everyone repackages), how to read that data yourself for free, and a straight, feature-by-feature comparison of what's genuinely free between Surfline and Boardcall.

What Surfline Actually Puts Behind the Paywall

Surfline's free tier isn't nothing — you get a basic surf report, today's conditions, and a short look ahead, with ads. What moves behind the subscription (historically called Surfline Premium; pricing and packaging change periodically, so check surfline.com for the current number) is specific, and worth naming plainly rather than hand-waving:

  • Extended forecast. Free accounts see roughly the next couple of days in real detail; the paid tier extends out to around two weeks using Surfline's proprietary LOTUS forecasting model.
  • Full live cams. The free tier gives you a short preview of each cam (historically a few minutes) before prompting you to subscribe or sit through ads; unlimited, ad-free access to the full cam network is a paid feature.
  • Buoy-calibrated spot detail (LOTUS). Surfline's model is tuned against their own buoy network for specific named breaks — this is a genuine technical edge, and the one part of the paywall that isn't just "the same free data behind a login."
  • No ads, plus cam replay and history. Minor next to the above, but part of the package.

What that means in practice: Surfline's free tier answers "what's the surf doing right now," but doesn't help you plan more than a day or two out, and does nothing at all with board choice — that's not a Surfline feature at any price.

The Free Data Every Surf Forecast Actually Runs On

Every surf forecasting product, Surfline included, ultimately starts from the same small set of public sources for the raw physics: wave and wind fields from global weather models, and tide predictions from national tidal authorities — in the US, NOAA's CO-OPS. What a paid product adds on top is packaging, spot-specific calibration, cams, and — in Surfline's case — the LOTUS model layered onto that public data.

If you want to look at the raw numbers yourself, for free, with no app and no login, these are the actual tools people use:

  • NOAA National Data Buoy Center (ndbc.noaa.gov) — real, live buoy readings: significant wave height, dominant period, wind speed and direction, straight from the ocean, no forecasting model in between.
  • NOAA CO-OPS Tide Predictions (tidesandcurrents.noaa.gov) — free tide tables and real-time water level for every US tide station.
  • Windy.com — a free, well-regarded wind and wave viewer that lets you compare several forecast models against each other.
  • Open-Meteo — a free, open marine and weather forecast API. A number of newer surf apps build on top of this rather than running a proprietary model from scratch.

The catch with all four: they hand you numbers, not a decision. A buoy reading of "1.1 m at 14 seconds" doesn't tell you whether that's worth the drive, or which board to grab on the way out the door — that translation is the actual job a surf app does, and it's closer to what Surfline's paywall is really selling than the underlying weather data itself, which nobody owns.

How to Read a Raw Forecast Like a Forecaster

If you're going the free-data route — a buoy reading, Windy, a generic marine forecast — here's how to translate the raw numbers into something useful, the same translation any surf report is doing behind the scenes.

Wave height on a buoy isn't what breaks on the beach. A buoy or offshore forecast reports significant wave height — a statistical average of the biggest third of waves in open water. That number is smaller than the breaking face height you'll actually see at the beach, and how much smaller depends heavily on swell period: longer-period swell shoals (builds up) far more dramatically as it hits shallow water than short-period wind chop of the same offshore height. Rough rule of thumb: a swell in the 6–9 second range barely builds at all by the time it breaks; one in the 12–16+ second range can build to noticeably more than its offshore reading, depending on the seafloor at that specific spot.

Surfers describe size in body-part terms rather than raw feet, because "3.4 feet" means nothing without context:

  • Under 1 ft — flat
  • 1–2 ft — ankle to knee high
  • 2–3 ft — knee to waist high
  • 3–4 ft — waist to chest high
  • 4–5 ft — chest to shoulder high
  • 5–7 ft — shoulder to head high
  • 7–9 ft — overhead
  • 9–12 ft — well overhead
  • 12+ ft — double overhead plus

Wind direction matters as much as speed. Wind blowing from the land toward the ocean (offshore) grooms the wave face and holds it up longer; wind blowing from the ocean toward the land (onshore) chops it up. Anything under about 4–5 knots barely matters either way; a strong onshore wind above roughly 15–20 knots will blow out good-looking swell regardless of what the height and period say on paper.

Period tells you how organized the energy is. Under 8 seconds is weak, disorganized wind swell; 8–11 seconds is a solid, workable mid-period swell; 12+ seconds is long-period groundswell — more power, cleaner faces, and it holds up longer than the raw height number alone would suggest.

Wetsuit and Board Sizing, With Real Numbers

Two more things a free-data setup leaves entirely to you — what to wear, and what to ride. Both have real, checkable numbers behind them.

Wetsuit thickness by water temperature (typical ranges used across major wetsuit brands; individual cold tolerance varies):

Water tempTypical suit
75°F+ (24°C+)Boardshorts or swimsuit, optional rash guard
68–75°F (20–24°C)Springsuit or 2mm top
63–68°F (17–20°C)3/2mm full suit
58–63°F (14–17°C)4/3mm full suit, boots optional
52–58°F (11–14°C)4/3mm or 5/4mm, boots and hood
44–52°F (7–11°C)5/4mm or 6/5mm hooded, boots and gloves
Below 44°F (below 7°C)6/5/4mm hooded steamer, thick boots and gloves

Board volume, roughly. Volume in liters is approximately length × width × thickness (in inches) × 0.0164 — that constant is just the cubic-inch-to-liter conversion — adjusted up or down for the board's shape. A longboard or soft-top with fuller rails and a flatter deck holds noticeably more foam than a shortboard with the same length, width, and thickness numbers, sometimes by 15–20%. As a rough starting target, a beginner typically wants a board close to their own body weight in liters (a bit under, in kilograms), scaling down substantially for an advanced or expert surfer in fast, powerful surf, where control matters more than flotation. Weak, short-period swell also asks for a bit more volume than the same wave height would if it had real push behind it — a board that floats fine in clean groundswell can feel dead in mushy wind swell of the same height.
