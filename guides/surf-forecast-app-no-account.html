The Case for a Surf Forecast App With No Account

Short answer: yes, a real surf forecast app with no account is possible. Swell, wind, tide, and even a board recommendation can all be pulled together and shown to you without a single email address, password, or "create account" screen standing in the way. If an app is making you sign up before it will tell you how big the waves are today, that's a business decision on their part, not a technical requirement.

The honest reason most surf apps want an account has nothing to do with giving you a better forecast. It's about attaching a name, an email, or a device identifier to whatever they collect, so it can be re-targeted later, funneled into a subscription pitch, or fed to an ad network. None of that improves the number on the screen that tells you whether it's worth paddling out this morning.

This page breaks down what a genuinely accountless surf app can and can't do, how to tell a real no-account app from one that just hides the signup wall behind a "skip" link, and exactly what does and doesn't leave your phone when you use one — using Boardcall, a free iPhone and iPad surf app with no login of any kind, as the running example throughout.

Why most surf apps want you to sign up (and why a forecast doesn't need it)

A surf forecast, at its core, is a lookup: pick a spot, fetch the swell height, period, and direction; fetch the wind speed and direction; fetch the tide. None of that requires knowing who you are. So when an app asks for an email address and a password before it'll show you a single number, that request is serving the company, not you. There are a few real reasons this pattern exists, worth naming plainly:

  • Ad targeting. An account, or even just a persistent login, lets an app attach your behavior to an identity it can sell into an ad network or use to build a profile over time.
  • Email-list building. Free apps with a paid tier often gate the free version behind a signup specifically to capture your email before you've decided whether to pay for anything — that address becomes a marketing channel whether or not you convert.
  • Legitimate cross-device sync. If an app genuinely lets you check saved spots on both your phone and a browser, an account is a real, defensible reason. This is the one case where the tradeoff can make sense.
  • Social or community features. Session logs shared with friends, "who's out now" maps, comment threads — these are inherently multi-user, so they need some form of identity. If you never touch those features, you're still carrying the account risk for them.

Notice what's missing from that list: nothing about producing a more accurate wave forecast requires knowing your name. If an app's core job is telling you what the surf is doing and what to ride, an account buys the company data — not you a better answer.

Three levels of "no account" — and how to tell which one you're getting

"No account" gets used loosely, and in practice it covers three very different situations. It's worth knowing which one you're actually looking at before you download anything and start setting up your spots and gear.

  1. Genuinely accountless. There is no sign-in screen anywhere in the app, ever. Your spots, your gear, your settings live only on your device, in local storage — not in a database row tied to your name or email on someone else's server. Delete the app and the data is gone with it, because there was never a second copy anywhere else to hold onto.
  2. An "optional" account that isn't really optional. The app technically has a "Continue as Guest" or "Skip" link, usually smaller and lower-contrast than the "Sign Up" button above it, but most of the real features — the multi-day forecast, saved spots, gear-based recommendations — are locked behind actually creating an account. This is the most common pattern, and it's designed to screenshot as accountless while functioning like any other login-gated app once you're inside.
  3. Accountless core, tracked anyway. No login screen, but the app ships with third-party analytics or advertising SDKs that generate and transmit a device identifier regardless — so there's technically no account, but there's still a persistent profile being built on the ad-tech side. You usually catch this by reading the app's official privacy label, not its marketing copy.

A fast way to test which tier an app actually falls into: open it for the first time and see how far you get toward an actual wave-height number before anything asks for an email address. If a "Sign Up" screen is the very first thing you see, with a barely-visible way past it, that's tier two — not tier one.

What data a surf app actually needs — and what's a red flag

A surf forecast app that genuinely needs nothing but a spot, and optionally some facts about you and your board, has a short, honest list of what should leave your phone:

DataWhat it's used forDoes it require identifying you?
Which spot you pickedFetching that location's wave, wind, and tide dataNo — it's coordinates from a built-in list, not a person
Skill level, weight, heightSizing a board recommendation and flagging conditions above your levelNo — it's arithmetic against numbers you already know about yourself
Your board's length, width, thicknessEstimating its volume to match it against today's conditionsNo — it describes an object you own, not you
Email address or phone numberNothing a forecast or a board recommendation needsThis is the one that should make you ask why it's being requested

Apple requires every app to disclose its data practices on its own App Store product page, under "App Privacy," and the three categories are worth actually understanding rather than skimming past. Data Not Collected means exactly that — nothing about you leaves the device. Data Not Linked to You means some data is collected, often something like a random, anonymous identifier used only for abuse prevention, but it isn't tied to your name, account, or Apple ID, so a breach or a legal request against that record can't be traced back to a specific person. Data Linked to You means the opposite: whatever's collected — device ID, location, purchase history — is explicitly tied to your identity, usually because there's an account or persistent login attaching it. A genuinely accountless surf app should land in one of the first two categories, never the third.

Practical red flags worth watching for: the app won't show a wave height until you've verified an email or phone number; the App Privacy section lists categories like Contacts or Browsing History, or marks Precise Location "Linked to You" and used for "Third-Party Advertising"; location permission is requested as "Always" rather than "While Using the App" for something that only needs to know which of a handful of named breaks you're near; or there's simply no plain-language way to see what a request to the app's own servers actually contains.

Real personalization without an account: wetsuit thickness and board volume

Skipping the login screen doesn't mean giving up real personalization — it just means the personalization runs on facts about you and your gear, not an identity tied to a server record. Two concrete examples any surf app can compute locally, no account required:

Wetsuit thickness by water temperature. This is standard guidance used across the wetsuit industry, and it's worth knowing on its own:

Water temperatureTypical suit
75°F and upBoardshorts or swimsuit; rash guard optional
68–75°FRash guard, 1–2mm top, or a shorty
65–68°F2/2mm or 3/2mm springsuit
62–65°F3/2mm fullsuit
58–62°F4/3mm fullsuit; boots below 60°F
52–58°F4/3mm or 5/4mm fullsuit, boots, gloves optional
48–52°F5/4mm hooded fullsuit, boots, gloves
43–48°F6/5mm hooded fullsuit, boots, gloves
Below 43°F6/5/4mm hooded fullsuit or drysuit; boots and gloves essential

Board volume. Volume, not length, is what actually predicts whether a board will work for you on a given day. If you don't have a manufacturer's spec, you can estimate it: multiply length, width, and thickness in inches to get cubic inches, convert to liters (1 cubic inch ≈ 0.0164 liters), then apply a shape factor for how full or pulled-in the rails and dome are — roughly 0.60 for a full longboard or soft-top nose to tail, down to about 0.47 for a narrow, pulled-in big-wave gun, with everyday shortboards, fish, and grovelers landing around 0.50–0.55. A 6'0" x 19.5" x 2.5" shortboard, for example, works out to roughly 29 liters with that method, which lines up with what most shapers actually publish for a board that size.

How much volume you need is a separate question from what a board has. A common starting point used in surf coaching: multiply your body weight in kilograms by a coefficient for your skill level.

Skill levelRough volume target
BeginnerBody weight (kg) × ~0.9
IntermediateBody weight (kg) × ~0.6
Advanced / expertBody weight (kg) × ~0.4

Then adjust from that baseline: small or weak surf needs more foam to catch anything, so nudge the target up. Genuinely big surf needs more volume too, which surprises people — a step-up or gun should carry more volume than your everyday shortboard, not less, because you need real paddle power to get into an overhead, fast-moving wave early. None of this arithmetic requires knowing your name. It requires knowing your weight, your skill level, and your board's dimensions — three facts you already have, that never needed to leave your device to be useful.

A 60-second checklist for vetting any "no account" surf app

Before you commit real time to setting up any app that claims to skip accounts — adding your spots, your gear, your skill level — run it through this in under a minute:

  • Open the app for the first time. Is there a login or signup screen at all? If so, is there a way past it, and how prominent is that way past it compared to the signup button?
  • Can you get a real, spot-specific forecast — not a demo or placeholder — before entering any personal contact information?
  • Check the App Store listing's "App Privacy" section before you download. Does it say "Data Not Collected" or "Data Not Linked to You," or does it list categories linked to your identity?
  • If the app recommends gear — a board, a wetsuit — does it ask for the physical facts it actually needs (your board's dimensions, your weight) without asking for anything that identifies you as a person?
  • If you uninstall the app, does anything about you persist somewhere you'd have to actively request deletion of? An app with no account, by definition, has nothing to delete on its end, because your data was never anywhere but your phone.
How Boardcall handles this

Boardcall is built to the first tier described above, not the second. There is no sign-in screen anywhere in the app, on iPhone or iPad, at any point — not at first launch, not behind a "Continue as Guest" link, not gating the six-day forecast, the worldwide spot map, or the recommended board. The one place Boardcall's design makes a real tradeoff: writing that day's plain-English prose is handled by an AI model, Claude, which is the single step that runs on a server rather than on your phone. The board recommendation itself isn't the AI's call — a deterministic physics calculation decides it: length x width x thickness to volume, adjusted for board shape, then matched against today's wave face height, period, wind, and your stated skill level and weight. Claude only writes the plain-English explanation of a pick the math already made. Everything else about you — your boards, your chosen spots, your skill level — stays stored locally on your phone, never in an account, the same as the genuinely accountless apps described above.

Quick answers

Does a surf forecast app need my location to work?

No. It needs to know which named spot you want a forecast for, which is different from tracking your real-time location. Boardcall, for example, uses a built-in list of 243 spots worldwide — you pick one, and the app fetches that spot's wave and wind conditions, plus tide data where a nearby station exists. It never requests GPS access at all.

Is an app really private just because it doesn't ask me to sign in?

Not automatically. Some apps skip the login screen but still collect and profile you by a device identifier through third-party ad or analytics SDKs. Check the App Store's "App Privacy" label specifically for "Data Not Collected" or "Data Not Linked to You" — not just the absence of a signup screen.

If Boardcall has no account, how does it remember my boards and spots between app opens?

The same way any app remembers preferences without a server: it's saved directly on your phone's local storage, not in a database tied to your name. Delete the app and that data goes with it, because there was never a second copy anywhere else.
