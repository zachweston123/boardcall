How Cold Is Too Cold to Surf Without a Wetsuit?

The short, honest answer: for most people, water in the low 60s Fahrenheit and colder needs at least a wetsuit, and anything below the high 60s starts to feel noticeably cold on a normal-length session. There's no single cutoff for exactly how cold is too cold to surf without a wetsuit — it depends on your own cold tolerance, how long you're planning to stay out, and what the wind and air temperature are doing while you're wet. But there are real, well-established ranges the surf industry has used for decades, and they're worth knowing before you paddle out in just boardshorts.

As a rough baseline: most surfers are comfortable bare-skin (or in just a rash guard) once water hits the mid-70s°F. Drop into the high 60s and a spring suit starts to make sense for anyone but the most cold-hardy. Below 60°F, you're in full-wetsuit territory, and once it dips under 50°F you're looking at a thicker suit plus boots, gloves, and a hood — regardless of how tough you think you are.

Below, we break down the exact thresholds by water temperature, what hypothermia actually looks like when it starts, and the other factors — wind, air temp, session length — that can make a "warm enough" number feel a lot colder in practice.

Water Temperature Thresholds: When You Actually Need a Wetsuit

Water temperature is the single biggest factor, but the transition from "fine" to "you need rubber" isn't a hard line — it's a gradient, and where you personally fall on it depends on body fat, experience, and how much you've acclimatized over a season. That said, these are the ranges most wetsuit brands, surf shops, and coaches use as a starting point:

  • 75°F and up: Boardshorts or a bikini is fine for most people. Some sensitive swimmers still want a thin rash guard for sun and rash protection, not warmth.
  • 68–75°F: Comfortable bare-skin for many, but a short-sleeve spring suit or a thicker rash guard extends session length for most people, especially on windy days.
  • Below 68°F: This is where most surfers start reaching for neoprene, even if they run warm. Below this range, going bare-skin isn't really a style choice anymore — it's a real risk if a session runs long.

If you're asking the question at all, you're probably somewhere in that 65–72°F zone where the answer is genuinely "it depends on you." Below 65°F, the honest answer for nearly everyone is: wear a suit.

Wetsuit Thickness by Water Temperature

Once you're past the bare-skin question, the next one is how thick. This is the range most surf shops and wetsuit manufacturers build their size charts around — treat it as a starting point, not gospel, since individual cold tolerance can shift you a category in either direction.

Water TempWhat Most Surfers Wear
75°F+ (24°C+)Boardshorts / swimsuit, no wetsuit needed
68–75°F (20–24°C)Rash guard or short-sleeve spring suit
62–68°F (17–20°C)2/2mm–3/2mm spring suit or full suit
52–62°F (11–17°C)3/2mm–4/3mm full suit, boots recommended
42–52°F (6–11°C)4/3mm–5/4mm full suit, plus boots, gloves, and a hood
Below 42°F (below 6°C)6/5mm suit or drysuit territory — boots, gloves, and hood are not optional

These numbers assume a normal-length session (an hour or two) in average wind. A longer sit in the lineup, a stiff onshore breeze, or a body that runs cold pushes you toward the thicker end of any range — there's no penalty for being one size warmer than you strictly need.

Hypothermia Signs Every Surfer Should Recognize

Cold water pulls heat out of your body roughly 25 times faster than cold air, which is why surfers can get into trouble at water temperatures that would feel merely brisk on land. Hypothermia doesn't announce itself — it creeps in as a series of small, easy-to-ignore signals.

  • Early stage: Uncontrollable shivering, numb fingers and toes, and a general urge to just get one more wave before heading in.
  • Middle stage: Clumsiness — fumbling your leash, missing pop-ups you'd normally stick, slower reactions paddling for a wave.
  • Later stage: Shivering that suddenly stops (a genuinely dangerous sign, not a good one), slurred speech, confusion, and poor judgment about your own condition.

The middle and later stages are the ones that matter most, because that's exactly when your ability to self-rescue is dropping. If you or anyone in your lineup starts fumbling basic tasks or seems confused, that's the moment to paddle in — not after one more set.

Water Temperature Isn't the Whole Story

Two sessions at the identical water temperature can feel completely different depending on what else is going on. A few factors that shift the real-world "cold" number:

  • Wind chill after you're wet. A stiff wind on wet skin between sets — or paddling back out after a wipeout — cools you faster than the water temperature alone suggests. Windy, overcast days call for a thicker suit than the chart alone would say.
  • Air temperature. Cold water under a cold, overcast sky is a different session than the same water temperature under a warm sun. Sun on a wet suit helps you recover heat between sets; a cold morning offshore breeze does the opposite.
  • Session length. A 20-minute dawn patrol and a two-hour session in the same water temperature are not the same exposure. If you know you're staying out a while, size up.
  • Individual tolerance. Body fat, fitness, and how much cold-water time you've logged this season genuinely change how a given temperature feels. Someone who surfs all winter builds real tolerance a summer-only surfer doesn't have.

None of this means the chart above is wrong — it means water temperature is the input, not the whole answer. The actual call on a given morning comes from combining the water reading with the wind, the sky, and how long you're planning to be out.

Quick answers

What's the coldest water you can surf in without a wetsuit?

There's no official cutoff, but most surfers start wanting at least a spring suit once water drops below the high 60s°F, and by the low 60s, bare-skin sessions become genuinely uncomfortable and risky for the vast majority of people. Individual cold tolerance varies, but if you're asking the question, the honest answer is usually "wear something."

Can you get hypothermia while surfing even in a wetsuit?

Yes, if the suit is too thin for the water temperature, has a leak, or you stay out much longer than the suit is rated for. A wetsuit slows heat loss, it doesn't stop it — matching thickness to actual water temperature, and getting out when you notice the early signs, still matters.

Is water temperature or air temperature more important for deciding what to wear?

Water temperature matters more for your core body heat, since you lose heat far faster in water than in air. But air temperature, wind, and sun still affect how a session feels overall — especially between sets and right after you get out — so both are worth checking, not just one.
