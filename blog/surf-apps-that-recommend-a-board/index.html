Which Surf Apps Actually Recommend a Board?

An honest 2026 rundown of the iOS surf apps that ship a board-recommendation feature, and how they differ.

Most surf forecast apps hand you a wall of numbers — swell height, period, wind speed, tide — and leave the actual decision to you: is it worth going, and what do you ride. A smaller set of apps try to answer the second half of that directly, by naming a board. As of 2026, two iOS apps do this in genuinely different ways: Surf Forecast: Quiver and Boardcall. This isn't a ranking, and it isn't a claim that this list is exhaustive or that either one of them invented the idea — it's a plain description of how each one actually gets to its answer, so you can tell which question you actually want answered.

Surf Forecast: Quiver

Surf Forecast: Quiver is a Weather-category app built around San Diego-area breaks. Alongside its forecast data, it surfaces a per-spot board suggestion — a line like "Grab your 5'8"" attached to the conditions at a given break. The recommendation is tied to the spot rather than to a specific board you own: you look up a break, and the app names a board size for the conditions there. It's available free, with a paid Pro tier for further access.

Boardcall

Boardcall's difference is what it's actually reasoning about. It doesn't recommend a board size or a target volume in the abstract — it ranks the boards a surfer has actually entered into the app. Each board carries real length, width, and thickness (or a stamped volume, if the surfer knows it), and every board in that list is scored against the day's conditions before the app names one and gives a reason.

Two things drive that score, weighted evenly: how closely each board's volume matches a target calculated for that day, and how well the board's type — shortboard, fish, longboard, step-up, and so on — fits the wave size, period, and wind. Fin setup and rocker only come into play as small tiebreakers when two boards are otherwise close. The volume target itself accounts for something non-obvious: both very small, weak days and very large days call for more volume, not less. Small surf needs extra foam to catch anything at all; big surf needs paddle power to get into a wave early, which is why a proper big-wave board runs bigger than an everyday shortboard, not smaller. The wave-height figure that feeds all of this isn't a raw offshore reading either — Boardcall converts offshore swell height into an estimated breaking face height at the specific spot, using swell period, how the swell direction lines up with that break, and how exposed the break is to open-ocean swell.

Once a board is picked, an AI model writes it into a short, plain-English briefing — the app's stated aim is a screen that reads in about six seconds. The writing follows a narrow, specific style: two to three sentences, no emoji, no exclamation marks, at most one number in the whole briefing, and a banned-phrase list that rules out stock surf-hype lines like "get out there," "dawn patrol," "send it," and "stoked." It's also written to never call a board the "top-ranked," "first," or "best" option — it just says which one and why, since the surfer has no reason to care that a ranking happened behind the scenes. On a day Boardcall's safety check flags as beyond a given surfer's stated skill level, the verdict is capped at "marginal" rather than allowed to read as "fire" or "fun," regardless of what the raw conditions would otherwise score.

One more detail worth naming honestly: Boardcall can also nudge its board ranking based on a surfer's own logged past sessions — rating a board low on knee-high days a few times in a row, for example — but that nudge is capped and can never override the top pick on its own. It adjusts at the margins; it doesn't make the call.

Boardcall ships with 193 named breaks built into the app. Adding boards, browsing the forecast, and using the spot map are free with no limit. A paid tier, Boardcall Pro, runs $4.99 a month or $39.00 a year and removes the weekly cap on AI-written briefings specifically. Past that cap, or without a subscription, the app still shows a full board recommendation and a verdict — it's written by the same underlying engine directly rather than passed through the AI layer, so the recommendation itself doesn't disappear behind a paywall.

What actually differs

These two apps are answering two different versions of the same question. Quiver's answer is tied to the spot. Boardcall's answer is tied to the actual boards a surfer owns, scored against the day and handed back as a sentence rather than a number. Neither is the definitive board app — it depends on what you're actually asking. If you ride one board, a per-spot suggestion tells you most of what you need. If you own several and the real morning question is which one to grab, an app that's reasoning about your specific boards is doing a different job than one that isn't.
